Every researcher I meet is quietly drowning in the same flood: too many papers, too little time, and a nagging sense that the one study that would change their argument is sitting unread in a database they never searched. In 2026, a wave of AI tools promises to fix this. Some genuinely help. Others quietly introduce new risks — fabricated citations, shallow synthesis, and an over-trust in software that does not actually understand your field.

This guide is written for the people I teach and work with: master's and PhD students, early-career scientists, lecturers, and research assistants. I reviewed six of the most widely used tools — **Scite, Jenni AI, Elicit, Consensus, ResearchRabbit, and Paperpal** — not from their marketing pages, but from the perspective of someone who has to defend a literature review, pass peer review, and uphold academic integrity.

::section 01 — Why AI Is Changing Research (and Where to Stay Cautious)

## The real pain points AI is responding to

The research lifecycle has not changed in principle — discover, read, evaluate, synthesise, write, manage — but its *scale* has. AI tools are spreading because they target genuine, well-documented friction:

- **Literature search is noisy.** Keyword search in Google Scholar or a database returns thousands of hits ranked by relevance you can't inspect. Finding the *right* 30 papers from 3,000 is the bottleneck.
- **Reading is overwhelming.** A serious review can mean hundreds of PDFs. Skimming for the one method or result you need, across all of them, is slow and error-prone.
- **Evaluating evidence is hard.** A paper with 800 citations might be heavily *disputed*. Raw citation counts hide whether later work supported or contradicted a claim.
- **Synthesis doesn't scale.** Pulling sample sizes, methods, and outcomes from 40 studies into one comparable table is tedious manual work.
- **Writing is a separate craft.** Non-native English speakers especially spend enormous effort on phrasing and structure that has nothing to do with the science.
- **Citation management is fiddly.** Styles, reference lists, and in-text citations consume hours and break easily.

:::pullquote -- The core principle
AI can compress the *mechanical* parts of research — searching, extracting, formatting, drafting. It cannot do the *judgement* — deciding what is true, what matters, and what your contribution is. Confuse the two and you will produce confident nonsense, faster.
:::

## Where AI genuinely helps — and where it doesn't

:::callout ✅ Where AI tools earn their place
**Discovery and triage** (finding candidate papers and mapping a field), **evidence evaluation** (seeing how a claim was cited), **structured extraction** (pulling comparable data from many papers), and **language polishing** (grammar, clarity, and academic tone). These are repeatable, checkable tasks where AI saves real hours.
:::

:::callout ⚠️ Where to keep your hands on the wheel
**Citations must be verified — every time.** Generative tools can fabricate plausible-looking references. **Coverage is never complete** — every tool indexes a subset of the literature and misses grey literature, books, and paywalled content. **Synthesis is not understanding** — an AI summary can miss the caveat that invalidates a study. And **academic-integrity rules vary** by institution and journal; using AI for writing may need disclosure. Treat outputs as a *draft to interrogate*, not an answer to copy.
:::

::section 02 — Scite.ai

## Scite — citation analysis that tells you *how* a paper was cited

**Overview.** Scite is a citation-analysis platform built around "Smart Citations." Instead of a raw count, it reads the text around each citation and classifies whether a later paper *supported*, *contrasted*, or merely *mentioned* the cited claim. It was founded in 2018 by Josh Nicholson, Yuri Lazebnik, and Sean Rife to make citations more meaningful.

**Primary purpose.** Evaluating the reliability and reception of a finding — *has this claim held up?*

### Key features

- **Smart Citations:** supporting / contrasting / mentioning classifications with the surrounding text snippet.
- **Citation context:** see the exact sentence where a paper was cited, not just that it was.
- **Reference checking / "Reference Check":** scan a manuscript's reference list for retracted or heavily contrasted sources.
- **Full-text search and dashboards** across a large index of articles.
- **Browser extension and Zotero/reference-manager integrations.**

### How it works

:::actions
**Step 1 — Create an account**
Sign up and (optionally) install the browser extension so Smart Citation badges appear on journal and PubMed pages.
---
**Step 2 — Search a claim or paper**
Enter a DOI, title, or research question. Scite returns the paper with its supporting/contrasting tallies.
---
**Step 3 — Inspect citation context**
Open the citing statements to read *why* each paper cited it — the actual sentences, grouped by stance.
---
**Step 4 — Check your own reference list**
Upload a draft to flag citations that have been retracted or widely contrasted before you submit.
---
**Step 5 — Export**
Save citation reports and integrate with your reference manager.
:::

### Research use cases

- **Grad students:** sanity-check a "famous" finding before building an argument on it.
- **PhD students:** find the contrasting literature your supervisor will inevitably ask about.
- **Faculty:** vet references in a manuscript or student thesis quickly; spot retractions.
- **Systematic reviewers:** assess how consistently an effect has been replicated.
- **Interdisciplinary researchers:** gauge the reception of a claim in a field they don't know well.

**Strengths.** The supporting/contrasting lens is genuinely novel and hard to get elsewhere — it surfaces controversy that citation counts hide. Reference checking against retractions is a real integrity safeguard. Deep integrations fit existing workflows.

**Limitations.** Smart Citation classification is automated and **can mislabel** stance, especially with hedged academic language — always read the snippet. Coverage depends on indexed full text, so **paywalled or non-indexed citations are missed**. It evaluates *reception*, not *correctness* — a contrasted paper isn't necessarily wrong. It does not read or synthesise papers for you.

**Learning curve:** *Beginner-friendly.* The badges are intuitive; the only skill is interpreting context honestly.
**Best for:** **Citation analysis and evidence reliability.**
**Alternatives:** Semantic Scholar (free citation context), Scholarcy, Dimensions.

::section 03 — Jenni AI

## Jenni AI — an AI writing assistant for long academic documents

**Overview.** Jenni AI is an AI-assisted writing environment aimed at students and researchers producing essays, literature reviews, and theses. It offers autocomplete, in-editor citations, and AI editing inside a document workspace.

**Primary purpose.** Accelerating *drafting* — overcoming the blank page and maintaining momentum in long-form academic writing.

### Key features

- **AI autocomplete** that continues your sentence or paragraph in context.
- **In-editor citations** with the ability to cite as you write and generate a reference list.
- **PDF upload and chat** to write grounded in your own sources.
- **AI edit and rephrase** for tone, concision, and clarity.
- **Export** to Word/LaTeX with formatted references.

### How it works

:::actions
**Step 1 — Create a document**
Start a project and set your topic, outline, or thesis structure.
---
**Step 2 — Upload sources**
Add PDFs so the assistant can draft grounded in your actual references rather than inventing them.
---
**Step 3 — Draft with autocomplete**
Write a lead sentence and let Jenni propose continuations; accept, edit, or reject each.
---
**Step 4 — Cite and edit**
Insert citations inline and use AI edits to tighten phrasing.
---
**Step 5 — Export**
Send the formatted document and reference list to Word or LaTeX.
:::

### Research use cases

- **Grad students:** structure and draft essays and literature reviews faster.
- **PhD students:** maintain momentum on thesis chapters; overcome writer's block.
- **Faculty:** draft non-critical text (reports, summaries) where speed matters.
- **Systematic reviewers:** assemble narrative sections once data is extracted elsewhere.
- **Interdisciplinary / non-native English researchers:** produce fluent academic English to then verify for accuracy.

**Strengths.** Excellent at reducing drafting friction and improving flow for non-native writers. Grounding drafts in uploaded PDFs reduces (but does not eliminate) fabrication. Citations-as-you-write saves real time.

**Limitations.** This is the highest-risk category for integrity. **AI-generated text can be generic, subtly wrong, or fabricate citations** — every claim and reference must be verified against the source. Many institutions and journals **require disclosure** of AI writing assistance, and some prohibit it. Over-reliance erodes the writing skill that doctoral training is meant to build. Use it to draft, never to think.

| Free | $0 | ~200 AI words/day — exploration only |
| Unlimited / Plus | ~$12/mo billed annually (≈$20 monthly; ~$144/yr) | Unlimited autocomplete, PDF uploads, AI edits, exports |

**Learning curve:** *Beginner-friendly.* It feels like a smarter word processor.

::section 04 — Elicit

## Elicit — structured evidence extraction across many papers

**Overview.** Elicit, built by the non-profit research lab Ought, is an AI research assistant that searches a large corpus of papers and **extracts structured data** — methods, sample sizes, outcomes — into a comparable table. It is the closest of these tools to a "research copilot" for empirical synthesis.

**Primary purpose.** Turning a research question into a screened, structured evidence table — the backbone of a literature review.

### Key features

- **Semantic search** across 125M+ papers (drawing on Semantic Scholar).
- **Find-papers workflow** that returns a table of results with one-line summaries.
- **Data extraction columns:** ask for "sample size," "intervention," "main finding," and Elicit fills the column per paper.
- **Automated reports / research summaries** synthesising across results.
- **Screening support** for inclusion/exclusion at scale.

### How it works

:::actions
**Step 1 — Ask a research question**
Enter a focused empirical question (Elicit works best on these, not vague topics).
---
**Step 2 — Review the results table**
Scan returned papers with auto-summaries; refine with filters.
---
**Step 3 — Add extraction columns**
Tell Elicit what to pull from each paper — outcomes, methods, populations — and it extracts into a grid.
---
**Step 4 — Screen and verify**
Include/exclude papers and **check extracted values against the source PDF**.
---
**Step 5 — Export**
Download the table (CSV) or a report into your review.
:::

### Research use cases

- **Grad students:** build a first evidence table for a coursework review in an afternoon.
- **PhD students:** scope a field and structure the empirical core of a chapter.
- **Faculty:** rapidly survey an adjacent literature for a grant or collaboration.
- **Systematic reviewers:** accelerate screening and extraction — *with* documented manual verification.
- **Interdisciplinary researchers:** extract comparable metrics across unfamiliar subfields.

**Strengths.** The standout tool for *empirical synthesis*. Structured extraction into tables is exactly what a literature review needs, and the generous free tier makes it accessible. Auto-summaries speed triage dramatically.

**Limitations.** **Extraction is not infallible** — values can be pulled incorrectly, so every cell used in analysis must be verified. Coverage is a subset of the literature and **misses books, grey literature, and non-indexed work**, so it is *not* a complete systematic-review engine on its own. Works best on well-formed empirical questions; weaker on theoretical or qualitative ones.

| Plan | Price (verified mid-2026) | Includes |
|---|---|---|
| Basic | $0 | Unlimited search & summaries; ~20 PDF extractions/mo; 5,000 one-time credits |
| Plus | $12/mo (≈$10/mo annual) | More extractions, automated reports |
| Pro | $49/mo | Bulk extraction, advanced workflows |
| Team / Enterprise | $79/user/mo / custom | Collaboration, admin |

**Learning curve:** *Intermediate.* The power is in column design and question framing — worth learning well.
**Best for:** **Literature review and structured evidence extraction.**
**Alternatives:** Consensus (Q&A), Scholarcy (summaries), Covidence (formal SR management).

::section 05 — Consensus

## Consensus — an evidence-based academic search engine

**Overview.** Consensus is an AI search engine that answers research questions directly from peer-reviewed papers. Its signature feature, the **Consensus Meter**, aggregates findings across studies to show how much the literature agrees on a yes/no empirical question.

**Primary purpose.** Getting a fast, citation-backed read on *what the evidence says* about a specific question.

### Key features

- **Question-led search:** ask "Does X cause Y?" and get synthesised, cited answers.
- **Consensus Meter:** a visual summary of agreement across studies (for suitable questions).
- **Study snapshots:** key details (population, design) surfaced per result.
- **Citation-grounded answers** that link straight to the source papers.

### How it works

:::actions
**Step 1 — Ask a yes/no or relationship question**
Phrase it empirically: "Is intermittent fasting effective for weight loss?"
---
**Step 2 — Read the synthesised answer**
Consensus summarises across papers with inline citations.
---
**Step 3 — Check the Consensus Meter**
See the proportion of studies that support, are unsure, or oppose.
---
**Step 4 — Open the underlying studies**
Click through to verify the claim and the study quality yourself.
---
**Step 5 — Capture citations**
Save the papers that matter into your reference manager.
:::

### Research use cases

- **Grad students:** orient quickly to what's known before reading deeply.
- **PhD students:** check whether a hypothesis is already settled or genuinely open.
- **Faculty:** answer evidence questions in teaching and public engagement.
- **Systematic reviewers:** scope the consensus before a formal protocol.
- **Interdisciplinary researchers:** get a credible first answer outside their expertise.

**Strengths.** Outstanding for empirical yes/no questions — fast, citation-backed, and honest about disagreement via the meter. Strong triage tool that always links to sources.

**Limitations.** The **Consensus Meter can oversimplify** — study quality and context matter more than a percentage. It is **not a writing or extraction tool**, and it struggles with theoretical, methodological, or open-ended questions. Coverage is limited to its index, so absence of evidence in Consensus is not evidence of absence.

**Learning curve:** *Beginner-friendly.* If you can ask a clear question, you can use it.
**Best for:** **Evidence-based question answering and rapid scoping.**
**Alternatives:** Elicit (extraction), Scite (citation stance), Semantic Scholar.

::section 06 — ResearchRabbit

## ResearchRabbit — visual literature discovery and citation mapping

**Overview.** ResearchRabbit is a free literature-discovery tool that builds **interactive citation graphs** from "seed" papers, revealing related work, references, and citing articles as a visual network rather than a flat list. It's often described as "Spotify for papers."

**Primary purpose.** Discovering the *shape* of a field and finding papers you'd never reach by keyword search.

### Key features

- **Seed-paper discovery:** add a few key papers and get a map of connected work.
- **Citation network graphs:** explore "earlier work," "later work," and "similar work" visually.
- **Author and timeline views** to track who and when.
- **Collections** to organise papers into projects, with **Zotero sync**.
- **Collaboration and alerts** for new related work.

### How it works

:::actions
**Step 1 — Add seed papers**
Start with 1–5 papers you know are central to your topic.
---
**Step 2 — Explore the graph**
ResearchRabbit maps references, citing papers, and similar work as a network.
---
**Step 3 — Follow the connections**
Click nodes to expand neighbourhoods and surface foundational and recent papers.
---
**Step 4 — Build collections**
Save relevant papers into a project; sync to Zotero.
---
**Step 5 — Set alerts**
Get notified when new connected work appears.
:::

### Research use cases

- **Grad students:** find the "must-read" classics and recent work in a new area fast.
- **PhD students:** map a field for the background chapter; spot gaps visually.
- **Faculty:** stay current via alerts; identify potential collaborators by network.
- **Systematic reviewers:** snowball sampling to ensure key papers aren't missed.
- **Interdisciplinary researchers:** see how two fields connect through shared citations.

**Strengths.** The best *free* discovery experience available, and the visual graph genuinely reveals structure that lists hide. Excellent for snowballing and for the "what am I missing?" anxiety. Zotero sync fits real workflows.

**Limitations.** It's **discovery only** — no synthesis, extraction, or writing. Graphs rely on citation data with **coverage gaps**, so newer or non-indexed work can be under-represented. There's **no quality filter** — popularity in the graph ≠ rigour. It's easy to lose hours down interesting rabbit holes.

| Plan | Price (verified mid-2026) | Includes |
|---|---|---|
| Free | $0 forever | Unlimited search across 280M+ articles, graphs, collections, Zotero sync, 1 project |
| RR+ | Local-economy pricing | Multiple projects, expanded settings |

**Learning curve:** *Beginner-friendly*, with an *intermediate* payoff once you read the graphs fluently.
**Best for:** **Literature discovery and citation mapping.**
**Alternatives:** Connected Papers, Litmaps, Inciteful.

::section 07 — Paperpal

## Paperpal — language and submission-readiness editing for academics

**Overview.** Paperpal, from Cactus Communications (the team behind Editage), is an academic writing and editing assistant focused on **language quality and submission readiness** — grammar, academic tone, and journal-style checks — purpose-built for researchers rather than general prose.

**Primary purpose.** Polishing a near-final manuscript to the standard reviewers and journals expect.

### Key features

- **Real-time academic language correction** tuned for scholarly writing.
- **Generative AI assistance** (rewrite, summarise, translate) in an academic register.
- **Submission-readiness checks:** 30+ technical checks before submission.
- **Plagiarism and AI-detection checks** (within limits).
- **Citations** in 10,000+ styles; Word and web integrations.

### How it works

:::actions
**Step 1 — Import your manuscript**
Work in the Word add-in or web editor with your near-complete draft.
---
**Step 2 — Accept language corrections**
Review real-time suggestions for grammar, tone, and academic phrasing.
---
**Step 3 — Run pre-submission checks**
Check structure, references, and journal-readiness with the 30+ checks.
---
**Step 4 — Screen integrity**
Run plagiarism and AI-detection passes as a final safeguard.
---
**Step 5 — Finalise**
Export the polished, correctly cited manuscript.
:::

### Research use cases

- **Grad students:** raise essays and theses to a professional language standard.
- **PhD students:** polish chapters and articles before supervisor or journal submission.
- **Faculty:** speed up editing of papers and grants; help students improve writing.
- **Systematic reviewers:** finalise the manuscript once synthesis is complete.
- **Interdisciplinary / non-native English researchers:** close the language gap that slows acceptance.

**Strengths.** The most *academically tuned* editor of this group — its suggestions respect scholarly register rather than flattening it. Submission-readiness and citation-style breadth are genuinely useful at the finish line. Strong fit for non-native English authors.

**Limitations.** It's an **editing tool, not a discovery or synthesis tool**. Generative suggestions still need author judgement — accepting them blindly can introduce errors or change meaning. Plagiarism and AI-detection are **aids, not guarantees**, and detection tools are imperfect. It improves *how* you write, not *what* you know.

::section 09 — The End-to-End Research Workflow

## Five stages, five tools, one project

::image{src=/blog/ai-tools-for-scientific-research-2026/research-workflow.jpg caption="The five-stage AI-assisted research workflow"}

Here is how these tools combine on a real review — say, *"the effect of spaced repetition on long-term retention."*

:::actions
**Stage 1 — Discover papers · ResearchRabbit + Consensus**
Drop 3 seminal spaced-repetition papers into ResearchRabbit to map the field and snowball outward; run the question through Consensus to see whether the effect is broadly supported and to grab high-signal studies.
---
**Stage 2 — Evaluate evidence · Scite**
For each candidate paper, check Scite's supporting vs. contrasting citations. Drop heavily contrasted or retracted work; flag the genuine controversies you'll need to address.
---
**Stage 3 — Extract information · Elicit**
Feed your screened set into Elicit and build an extraction table: population, retention interval, effect size, study design. Verify each cell against the PDF.
---
**Stage 4 — Write the draft · Jenni AI**
With your evidence table in hand, draft the synthesis in Jenni — grounded in your uploaded sources — accepting autocompletions only where they match your data, and citing as you write.
---
**Stage 5 — Polish the manuscript · Paperpal**
Run the full draft through Paperpal for academic language, citation styling, and the 30+ submission-readiness checks before it reaches your supervisor or the journal.
:::

:::pullquote -- On combining tools
Discovery, evaluation, and extraction are where AI multiplies you. Writing and polishing are where it assists you. Judgement stays yours at every stage — the workflow is a scaffold, not an autopilot.
:::

::section 12 — Frequently Asked Questions

## FAQ

**Are AI research tools allowed in academic work?**
Generally yes for *discovery, search, and editing*, but policies on *AI writing* vary by institution and journal and often require disclosure. Always check your university's academic-integrity policy and the target journal's AI policy before submitting.

**Can these tools replace reading papers?**
No. They help you *find, triage, and extract* faster, but summaries and extractions can miss the caveat that changes everything. Read the papers your argument depends on.

**Do AI tools hallucinate citations?**
Generative writing tools can fabricate plausible-looking references. Discovery and citation tools (ResearchRabbit, Scite, Consensus, Elicit) are grounded in real indexes, but you must still verify every citation against the original source.

**Which tool is best for a systematic review?**
Elicit for screening and structured extraction, plus ResearchRabbit for snowball sampling. For formal protocol management and PRISMA compliance, pair them with a dedicated SR platform like Covidence.

**What's the cheapest way to start?**
ResearchRabbit (free) for discovery and Consensus's free tier or student plan for evidence questions. You can run a serious review on close to zero budget.

**Are my uploaded papers and data private?**
Check each tool's data-use and privacy policy before uploading unpublished work or sensitive data, and avoid uploading confidential or embargoed material to any third-party AI service.
