There is a quiet crisis unfolding in academia. Brilliant researchers are producing world-class work — and almost nobody outside their department knows it exists. Their papers sit behind paywalls, their teaching is seen by 40 students per semester, and their expertise is invisible to the 4 billion people online who could benefit from it. Meanwhile, a 24-year-old with a laptop and a camera is building a YouTube channel on machine learning with 200,000 subscribers. The world does not reward obscurity. It rewards visibility.

The good news: you don't need to become an influencer. You need a system. And in 2026, AI makes that system something you can run in under three hours a week.

::section 01 — Choose Your Niche

## Teaching CS in China? You Have a Rare Perspective the World Wants

Before you open a camera app, answer one question: <mark>what can you teach that nobody else can?</mark> For a university lecturer teaching ML and Deep Learning to Chinese CS students in English, the answer is hiding in plain sight. You sit at the intersection of three things the internet deeply craves: rigorous technical depth, the rare skill of making complex ideas accessible to non-native English speakers, and real classroom experience turning confused students into competent practitioners.

The most overcrowded YouTube lanes are *"Python for beginners"* and *"what is ChatGPT."* The genuinely underserved lanes — where you can build a loyal audience quickly — are **step-by-step mathematical walkthroughs** of transformer attention, backpropagation, and CNNs with actual numbers worked through by hand. Students from Egypt to Indonesia to Brazil are desperate for this content. They don't want vibes. They want someone who will sit with them in the algebra.

::section 02 — The Content Stack

## One Lecture. Five Pieces of Content. Zero Extra Hours.

The biggest mistake academics make is treating each platform as a separate creative effort. The correct model is a **content pyramid**: one core asset at the top, repurposed downward by AI. Here is the exact workflow:

:::actions
**Record your lecture (once)**
Record a 12–20 minute YouTube video explaining one concept with full numerical worked examples — the kind you already do in class. No studio needed. Good light, a decent mic ($30 works), and a tablet for handwriting equations. This is your core asset.
---
**Transcript → LinkedIn post (AI, 10 minutes)**
Paste your transcript or notes into Claude or ChatGPT with the prompt: *"Write a 200-word LinkedIn post for an academic audience based on this explanation of [topic]. Open with a provocative question. End with what beginners most misunderstand."* Post once a week. LinkedIn rewards academic credibility heavily.
---
**Clip the aha-moment for Shorts/Reels (5 minutes)**
Every lecture has one 45-second moment where everything clicks. That's your YouTube Short, your Instagram Reel, your TikTok. Use CapCut or Opus Clip to auto-detect and trim it. This feeds the algorithm and funnels people to your full video.
---
**Turn your slides into a personal site post (AI, 15 minutes)**
Upload your slides or notes to Claude and ask it to generate a structured blog post. Publish it on a simple site (Notion, GitHub Pages, or Hashnode — all free). This makes you discoverable via Google search for years after you post it.
---
**Thread version for X / Twitter (AI, 5 minutes)**
Academic Twitter — now X — still has an active ML research community. A 6-tweet thread of your key insight gets more reach than a full paper announcement. Let AI draft it; you edit for your voice.
:::

:::callout 🎯 Your Highest-ROI YouTube Topics Right Now
**Transformers with full numerical attention walkthrough** — almost no videos do this with actual numbers. Students worship them.

**ResNet / EfficientNet architecture explained from scratch** — the "why" behind skip connections is chronically under-explained.

**"My students were confused by X — here's how I fixed it"** — classroom confession format. Extremely shareable among educators.

**Building ML projects step by step in Keras/TensorFlow** — not "here's the code," but the thinking process. Rare and valuable.
:::

::section 03 — The Personal Site

## Your Academic Home Base: Build It Once, Let It Work for You

A personal academic website is not vanity — it is <mark>your permanent, algorithm-independent address on the internet</mark>. Hiring committees, collaborators, and journalists search for you. If they find nothing, they move on. If they find a clean site with your research, teaching philosophy, course previews, and a few videos, you become real to them.

You don't need to hire a developer. Use Claude to generate a React or Next.js portfolio in an afternoon. Include: a short bio written in first person (not academic third), a teaching section with one free preview video per course, a publications list with plain-English summaries written by AI, and a contact form. Update it quarterly. That's it.

::section 04 — Consistency Without Burnout

## The Only Schedule That Actually Works for Busy Faculty

Consistency is the real problem — not capability. You already know more than enough. The solution is to batch ruthlessly and lower the bar for "done." One solid video per month is better than four anxious ones that never get uploaded. Set a *minimum viable post* rule: if it took you 45 minutes and teaches one true thing clearly, it ships. AI handles the polish. You provide the substance.

Build a simple content calendar: one YouTube video per month, four LinkedIn posts per month (one per week from AI-repurposed lecture notes), and one site update per quarter. That is a professional online presence that compounds over time. In two years, you will have 24 videos, 96 LinkedIn posts, and a searchable site — all from work you were already doing in the classroom.

The lecture you give on Tuesday is already content. You just haven't pressed record yet.
:::

::section 05 — The Long Game

## This Is Not About Fame. It's About Not Disappearing.

The uncomfortable truth is that academic institutions are no longer the only arbiters of expertise. Students, employers, and funding bodies now triangulate credibility across journals, Google search, and YouTube watch-time. A researcher with 5,000 YouTube subscribers and a clean personal site is, in practical terms, more legible to the world than one with 50 citations and no web presence.

You do not need to perform. You do not need to be entertaining. You need to be *findable*, *credible*, and *clear*. AI handles the packaging. Your years of teaching and research are the substance. Start with one video. Write one LinkedIn post from your last lecture. Register your name as a domain. The compounding begins the moment you do.

The professors who are invisible in five years will not have been less talented. They will simply have never pressed record.
