There is a quiet crisis unfolding in academia. Brilliant researchers are producing world-class work — and almost nobody outside their department knows it exists. Their papers sit behind paywalls, their teaching is seen by 40 students per semester, and their expertise is invisible to the 4 billion people online who could benefit from it. Meanwhile, a 24-year-old with a laptop and a camera is building a YouTube channel on machine learning with 200,000 subscribers. The world does not reward obscurity. It rewards visibility.

The good news: you do not need to become an influencer. You need a system. And in 2026, AI makes that system something any academic can run in under three hours a week.

:::stats
87% — Hiring committees Google candidates before shortlisting
3 hrs — Per week with AI assistance is enough to build meaningful presence
10× — More collaboration inquiries reported by faculty with active profiles
:::

::section 01 — The Visibility Gap

## The Professor Who Knows More Than Google Can Find

::image{src=/blog/invisible-in-5-years/01.jpg caption="Teaching Machine Learning and Deep Learning to undergraduate students at University. The same explanations that help a classroom of 40 students can reach 40,000 learners online — the content is identical. Only the medium changes."}

Consider a thought experiment. Two researchers have identical CVs — same publications, same h-index, same teaching record. One has a personal website, a modest YouTube channel, and posts once a week on LinkedIn. The other has nothing online. A hiring committee, a potential collaborator, and a PhD applicant all Google both names. What happens next is not subtle.

The visibility gap is not about fame. It is about <mark>being findable by the right people at the right moment</mark>. A student looking for a supervisor in chaos theory. A journal editor seeking a reviewer. A conference organizer building a panel on explainable AI. These people search. The question is whether they find you — or someone less qualified who simply made the effort to be found.

:::pullquote -- On the nature of academic visibility
The expertise already exists. The only question is whether the people who need it can find it.
:::

::section 02 — Why Your Perspective Is Rare

## The Intersection Nobody Else Occupies

Before opening a camera app, answer one question: <mark>what can you teach that nobody else can?</mark> For a university lecturer teaching ML and Deep Learning to international CS students in English, the answer is hiding in plain sight. The intersection of rigorous technical depth, the skill of making complex ideas accessible to non-native English speakers, and real classroom experience turning confused students into competent practitioners — that combination is genuinely rare online.

The most overcrowded YouTube lanes are *"Python for beginners"* and *"what is ChatGPT."* The genuinely underserved lanes — where a loyal audience builds quickly — are **step-by-step mathematical walkthroughs** of transformer attention, backpropagation, and CNNs with actual numbers worked through by hand. Students from Egypt to Indonesia to Brazil search for exactly this content. They do not want vibes. They want someone who will sit with them in the algebra.

Real classroom experience is the credential that no generative AI can replicate. A tutorial written by a language model does not know which misconception trips students up at step three. A professor who has taught that concept forty times does.

::section 03 — The Content System

## One Lecture. Five Pieces of Content. Zero Extra Hours.


::image{src=/blog/invisible-in-5-years/02.jpg caption="The content repurposing system: one lecture becomes a YouTube video, a LinkedIn post, a blog article, a Short, and a newsletter — all from the same 90 minutes of preparation already done for class."}


The biggest mistake academics make is treating each platform as a separate creative effort requiring separate preparation. The correct model is a **content repurposing system**: one core asset at the top, distributed downward with AI assistance. Here is the exact workflow:

:::actions
**Record your lecture (once)**
Record a 12–20 minute YouTube video explaining one concept with full numerical worked examples — the kind already done in class. No studio needed. Good light, a decent microphone ($30 works), and a tablet for handwriting equations. This becomes the core asset.
---
**Transcript → LinkedIn post (AI, 10 minutes)**
Paste the transcript into Claude with the prompt: *"Write a 200-word LinkedIn post for an academic audience based on this explanation of [topic]. Open with a provocative question. End with what beginners most misunderstand."* Post once a week. LinkedIn rewards academic credibility more than any other platform.
---
**Clip the aha-moment for Shorts and Reels (5 minutes)**
Every lecture has one 45-second moment where everything clicks. That is your YouTube Short, Instagram Reel, and TikTok. Use CapCut or Opus Clip to auto-detect and trim it. This feeds the algorithm and funnels viewers to the full video.
---
**Turn slides into a site post (AI, 15 minutes)**
Upload slides or notes to Claude and ask it to generate a structured blog post. Published on a personal site, this becomes searchable via Google for years — long after the lecture is over and the students have graduated.
---
**Thread version for X / Twitter (AI, 5 minutes)**
The ML research community on X is still active and influential. A 6-tweet thread of the key insight reaches more people than a formal paper announcement. AI drafts it. You edit for your own voice.
:::

:::callout 🎯 The Highest-ROI Topics for ML Educators Right Now
**Transformers with full numerical attention walkthrough** — almost no videos do this with actual numbers. It is the single most requested topic in ML education comments.

**ResNet / EfficientNet architecture from scratch** — the mathematical reason skip connections work is chronically under-explained in every existing tutorial.

**"My students were confused by X — here is how I fixed it"** — the classroom confession format. Extremely shareable among educators globally.

**Building ML projects step by step** — not just the code, but the reasoning behind every architectural decision. This is what separates useful tutorials from code dumps.
:::

::section 04 — The Platform

## Own Your Address on the Internet


A personal academic website is not vanity. It is <mark>an algorithm-independent, permanent address on the internet that you control</mark>. Social media accounts can be deactivated, university profile pages can be restructured, and email addresses change with every new appointment. A personal domain does not.

::image{src=/blog/invisible-in-5-years/03.jpg caption="drirfanahmad.com — research, teaching, publications, and blog in one place. When someone searches for your name, this is what they find instead of nothing."}

Hiring committees, collaborators, and journalists search for names. If they find nothing, the interaction ends. If they find a clean site with research outputs, teaching materials, course previews, and a few videos, a person becomes professionally real to them in a way that a CV attached to an email never achieves.

The key insight is that a personal site does not require ongoing maintenance to justify its existence. A well-structured site built once — with publications, a bio, contact information, and two or three pieces of content — compounds in search visibility for years without additional effort.

:::pullquote -- On owning your platform
Social media platforms change their algorithms. Universities restructure their websites. Your name dot com stays exactly where you put it.
:::

::section 05 — The Schedule

## The Only Content Calendar That Works for Busy Faculty

Consistency is the real obstacle, not capability. The solution is to batch ruthlessly and lower the threshold for what counts as "done." One solid video per month is more valuable than four anxious drafts that never get uploaded.

A sustainable minimum: one YouTube video per month, four LinkedIn posts per month drawn from AI-repurposed lecture notes, and one site update per quarter. That is a professional online presence that compounds. In two years: 24 videos, 96 LinkedIn posts, and a searchable site — all produced from work that was already being done in the classroom.

The lecture exists. The expertise exists. The only missing piece is pressing record.

::section 06 — The Long Game

## This Is Not About Fame. It Is About Not Disappearing.

Academic institutions are no longer the sole arbiters of expertise. Students, employers, and funding bodies now triangulate credibility across journal databases, Google search results, and YouTube watch-time simultaneously. A researcher with a modest YouTube channel and a clean personal site is, in practical terms, more discoverable to the people who matter than one with a stronger publication record and no web presence.

This is not an argument for performing or entertaining. It is an argument for being *findable*, *credible*, and *clear*. AI tools handle the packaging and distribution. Years of teaching and research provide the substance that no algorithm can manufacture.

The professors who are invisible in five years will not have been less talented. They will simply have never pressed record.
